## ⚖️ Morocco rewrites the rulebook for lawyers — and criminalizes faking it

A sweeping new law, **Law 66.23 on the organization of the legal profession**, has been enacted and published, replacing the framework that governed who can become a lawyer in Morocco and how they must operate. It reached the Official Gazette after Morocco's Constitutional Court reviewed a referral challenging its constitutionality and said it could not rule on the merits, effectively letting the text stand as written.

The law affects anyone who deals with lawyers in Morocco — which is to say, nearly everyone who ever needs a contract reviewed, a case defended, or a dispute settled, including Moroccans abroad handling property or inheritance matters back home. Key changes:

- **Who can become a lawyer**: candidates must be Moroccan (or a national of a country with a reciprocal agreement), aged between 21 and 45 at the time of the entrance exam, hold at least a master's degree in law or Islamic jurisprudence, and pass a national entrance exam for the lawyer-training institute.
- **Training**: a 24-month traineeship is now required — 20 months under a supervising lawyer plus 4 months placed in a public administration or institution.
- **Conflicts of interest**: lawyers are now explicitly barred from combining legal practice with commercial activity, running a company, holding public or judicial office, working other liberal professions, or taking salaried jobs.
- **Money transparency**: legal fees above **10,000 dirhams** must now be paid by check or electronic transfer — cash is no longer allowed above that threshold — and lawyers must issue dated, numbered, signed receipts for any funds they handle. Mandatory professional liability insurance is also introduced.
- **New criminal penalties**: impersonating a lawyer or fraudulently wearing a lawyer's robe now carries **2 to 4 years in prison and a fine of 20,000 to 50,000 dirhams**. Practicing law without authorization carries **3 months to 2 years in prison** plus the same fine range, unless a harsher penalty already applies elsewhere.
- A full disciplinary system is codified, with regional bar councils, disciplinary panels, and appeal rights before the relevant Court of Appeal.

*Source: Dahir 1.26.75 enacting Law 66.23, dated 5 Rabi' al-Awwal 1448 (18 August 2026).*

## 🏛️ Constitutional Court forces a tweak to who can be appointed to Morocco's top state jobs

A separate organic law amends the list of "senior positions" (مناصب عليا) that the Moroccan government appoints via Council of Government under the constitution's rules on high-level state appointments. The Constitutional Court ruled that adding a post described in the text as **"general judicial curators"** to that list was unconstitutional — but ruled the flaw could be surgically removed without blocking the rest of the law. The government complied: the post has been struck from the list before publication, and everything else in the amendment stands as approved by both houses of parliament.

This is ultimately a story about institutional guardrails: an attempt to bring a specific senior judicial-administration post into the government's political appointment process was blocked by the country's top constitutional watchdog.

*Source: Dahir 1.26.74 enacting organic law 026.26, dated 5 Rabi' al-Awwal 1448 (18 August 2026).*

## 💧 Settat gets a major drinking-water upgrade, drawing from Casablanca's water network

The government has formally declared it a matter of public interest to reinforce the drinking-water supply of **Settat**, drawing additional water from the Dourat canal that runs through the Dar Bouazza area of Casablanca. To make it happen, the state is expropriating the land parcels needed in the Lahouazate commune, Settat province, and has handed execution of the project — including the power to carry out the expropriation — to **ONEE**, Morocco's National Office of Electricity and Drinking Water.

*Source: Decree 2.26.555, dated 19 Safar 1448 (3 August 2026).*
